# Human Judging Opens a Mid-Market Styling Lane

*Research as of 21 Nov 2026. Legal and regulatory points are analysis for planning, not legal advice. Items marked [lawyer] need review by an IP, advertising or privacy attorney. Figures marked (estimate) or (unverified) come from third-party trackers, informal sources or my own arithmetic.*

No live game combines real mid-market clothes, real 1:1 prices, body-type-specific client briefs and player voting. That lane is open, and the evidence for its core thesis is strong: in a 2026 survey of 6,126 young Americans, **85% use AI but only 2% trust it for fashion advice**, while **34% trust friends** ([RetailBoss](https://retailboss.co/pacsun-youth-report-finds-a-gap-between-ai-use-and-fashion-trust/)). Meanwhile, every well-funded AI player (Alta, Daydream, Google, Amazon, OpenAI, Pinterest) is competing on generating looks, and none is building structured human judging. The open lane is narrow, though. Western fashion styling games are a mature, slowly shrinking niche: Covet Fashion, the closest comparable, now peaks at roughly **$263K a week in US revenue**, about $8–12M a year (estimate), down from about $67M a year in 2019 ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/2025-q2-unified-top-5-fashion%20games-revenue-us-6011f531241bc16eb85bc2b8); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/is-glu-mobiles-45-million-crowdstar-acquisition-already-paying-off)). The money math favors in-app purchases (IAP) by one to two orders of magnitude over affiliate links, so the concept's "IAP primary, affiliate secondary" instinct is right. The biggest risks are not competitors. They are retention (the median mobile game keeps under 1% of players at day 30), the cost of rendering every item on several body types, brand permission to modify product images, and a regulatory wave that makes 16+ far less of a safe harbor than it looks. Defensibility will come from the voting community, a voter-reputation system and brand catalog rights, not from the mechanics, which are easy to copy.

## No live game combines real mid-market items, real prices and voting

The category splits into four types, and each covers only part of the concept. Real-brand challenge-and-vote games (Covet, Drest) have the closest mechanics, but Covet sits in late-life decline under EA and Drest has moved to luxury brand sponsorships. Roblox's Dress to Impress proved that a timed theme, a runway and peer star-voting form a viral loop for teen girls, but it uses fantasy items with no real prices. Chinese gacha dress-up games (the Nikki series) win on production budget. Avatar social platforms (Zepeto, Roblox brand worlds) sell brand activations, not styling skill.

Three patterns matter for the concept. First, **voting integrity is the most common complaint across titles**: spite and clique voting in Dress to Impress, closet-value tiers in Covet. Whoever fixes fair judging owns the category's top pain point. Second, Gen Z and female players organize against monetization changes they see as greedy, as the Nikki "girlcott" showed, so pricing promises like "never pay-to-win votes" have to hold up in practice. Third, **body diversity arrived late and is thin**. Drest added different body sizes only in its 2024 relaunch, and Covet offers varied body types and ethnicities ([Apple editorial](https://apps.apple.com/us/story/id1497022178)). Nobody has made body type the core of the challenge brief, which is the concept's clearest product differentiator. No game runs explicit "dupe" or recreate-the-trend challenges at real 1:1 retail prices. Covet's budget caps are the nearest analog.

## AI giants race to generate looks while nobody owns judging them

The AI styling field is crowded and well funded, but it is all on the generation side. **Alta** raised a $20M Series A in Oct 2026 and has created 20M avatars and 700M outfits, but it is pivoting to selling AI to retailers such as Rent the Runway and Gap ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/22/alta-ai-fashion-app-series-a-20-million-forerunner-rent-the-runway/)). **Daydream** (a $50M seed) launched an autonomous shopping agent on 18 Nov 2026 ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/18/daydream-personal-shopping-agent-ai-assistant)). **Whering** reached 10M mostly Gen Z users and raised $7M from eBay Ventures and Google ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/07/07/whering-lands-7m-as-digital-wardrobe-platform-reaches-10m-users/)). Google is shutting its standalone Doppl try-on app and moving try-on into Search ([Google Labs Help](https://support.google.com/labs/answer/16537062?hl=en)). Pinterest launched an 18+ AI shopping assistant in Oct 2025 ([Pinterest Newsroom](https://newsroom.pinterest.com/news/pinterest-assistant-revolutionizing-the-way-you-shop-online/)).

| "Rate my outfit" apps (Clueless Style, WinFIT, Slay or Nay) | Mostly AI scoring; some friend polls | Small-scale peer polls | Supply side is testing the idea; no one has scale |

The trust data is the strongest support for the thesis that AI can style you but humans validate. Beyond Pacsun's 2% figure, YPulse found friends were the top influence on Gen Z clothing purchases ([YPulse](https://www.ypulse.com/article/2021/09/23/this-chart-shows-the-biggest-influences-on-gen-z-millennials-fashion-purchases/)). Surveys conflict on wording, though: 40% of UK Gen Z say they would *use* an AI that learns their style ([YouGov UK](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55973-ai-in-fashion-retail-what-are-uk-shoppers-willing-to-use-it-for)). The best reading is that Gen Z will use AI but don't trust it. That supports a design where AI (or the catalog) proposes and humans judge. One caveat cuts against the concept: the trusted judges are **friends and family, not strangers**. Influencers sit at only 10.8%, and anonymous strangers aren't measured at all.

Polyvore's history adds a monetization warning. Yahoo paid about $230M for it in 2015, and SSENSE bought its assets in 2018 and shut it the same day to redirect traffic ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/04/05/polyvore-is-shutting-down-after-being-acquired-by-fashion-e-commerce-site-ssense)). Users mourned the creative community, not the shopping: one put it as "no one used Polyvore to shop. We used Polyvore to create" ([Nylon](https://www.nylon.com/articles/polyvore-shutdown-ssense-acquirement)). A styling game's audience will be creators and competitors first and shoppers a distant second.

## A $10M-scale niche where IAP outearns affiliate roughly 40 to 1

The macro market is large but flat. Newzoo puts 2025 mobile games at **$113.3B** ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/global-games-revenue-surpasses-200bn-for-the-first-time-mobile-generates-113bn/)). Sensor Tower, which counts only the App Store and Play Store, puts IAP at **$82B (+1%)**, and H1 2026 IAP fell 2% while downloads dropped about 12% ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/press/press-release-sensor-tower-state-of-gaming-gaming-drove-52-billion-downloads-82b-iap-revenue-on-mobile-and-12b-premium-revenue-on-steam); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/mobile-gaming-iap-dipped-2-as-ad-spend-climbed-in-h1-2026/)). The relevant number is the niche: the top two US fashion games, Covet and SuitU, each peak around **$100–260K a week**. No genre-total figure exists.

Gen Z spending trends favor the mid-market positioning. BCG projects Gen Z and Gen Alpha will account for 40% of fashion spending within a decade ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/press/29october2025-gen-z-gen-alpha-drive-fashion-spending)). PwC found Gen Z cut spending 13% in early 2025 and **82% plan to buy cheaper alternatives** ([PwC](https://pwc.com/us/en/industries/consumer-markets/library/gen-z-consumer-trends.html)). That is the "dupe" and "10 things under $200" mindset. It also means smaller baskets and smaller commissions.

The one real-world conversion data point supports these low affiliate numbers. A French Connection exclusive dress in Covet drove **110,000+ click-throughs but only $12,000 in sales in 10 days** ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2015/09/09/covet-fashion-mobile-game/)). That is about 11 cents of sales per click, and roughly a cent of commission at a 10% rate. Drest's move from Farfetch affiliate commerce to brand sponsorships points the same way.

Brand money exists but is unproven at small scale. Gamefam counted **600+ brand campaigns on Roblox in 2025, 60+ of them in fashion** ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/411480/)). Agency estimates put a single item drop at low five figures and a custom world at six to seven figures (unverified) ([Influencers-Time](https://www.influencers-time.com/roblox-brand-playbook-build-creator-experiences-that-drive-r/)). Brands complain that ROI measurement in games is weak ([The Interline](https://www.theinterline.com/?p=27517)). That gap is exactly what a game with real items, real prices and click data could fill. Trend forecasters like WGSN and Heuritech sell custom enterprise contracts at undisclosed prices ([Heuritech](https://heuritech.com/pricing/)), and no game has been found selling player style data to brands. That revenue line is speculative until the game reaches scale.

## Brand permission, not product feeds, is the real supply chain

Every successful styling product ran on direct brand deals, not scraped photos. Covet used unpaid "exposure" swaps: brands get publicity and Covet gets to use their names, with players buying "digitally rendered illustrations" of in-season collections ([Glossy](https://www.glossy.co/fashion/fashion-brands-are-linking-with-gaming-apps-to-acquire-customers); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2015/09/09/covet-fashion-mobile-game/)). Drest got most of its assets through Farfetch as a commercial partner. Polyvore let users "clip" images from anywhere and was sued for it by a photo agency in *BWP Media v. Polyvore* ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2017/02/bwp-media-v-polyvore-its-all-about-control)).

Affiliate feeds (Awin, CJ, Rakuten, Impact) do give you what the real-price mechanic needs: name, brand, price, stock status and a deep link. But the image licence is narrow. Merchant terms on Awin, for example, prohibit **modifying** imagery ([Awin Lucy & Yak](https://ui.awin.com/merchant-profile-terms/127047/brand)). Cutting a product out of its background and putting it on an avatar almost certainly counts as modification [lawyer]. The workable model is **feeds for prices and links, plus brand opt-in for the wearable art**.

| Asset approach | Cost per item | Legal exposure | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2D cutout plus warp from product photo | Cents (automated) | Highest: modifies the brand's copyrighted photo | Only with written brand consent |
| AI virtual try-on API | About $0.04–0.10 per image ([fal.ai](https://fal.ai/learn/tools/best-virtual-try-on-apis-2026)) | Derivative of the source photo; the game may not own strong rights in the output [lawyer] | Fast prototyping, partner items |
| House-style illustration (Covet model) | No benchmark found | Lower copyright reliance, but still depicts brand designs and marks | Best default with brand opt-in |
| 3D (CLO3D) | About $39–199+ per item from studios ([BinaryCloth](https://binarycloth.com/clo3d-apparel-design-cost-guide/)) | Low with brand files | Hero or sponsored items only |

Body-type-specific challenges multiply this cost. Every garment has to be fitted on every body you offer, so 500 items on 5 bodies is 2,500 assets. That is probably why competitors were slow on size inclusion, and it is the single biggest production decision. Prices also go stale. Feeds refresh anywhere from every 5 minutes to every 24 hours ([Koongo](https://www.koongo.com/channels/cj-com/)), so show "price at last check" with a timestamp, and keep sold-out items wearable as collectibles.

### The challenge themes that carry the most legal risk

| Challenge idea | Risk | Evidence | Safer framing [lawyer] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Event tie-ins ("Coachella look," "Met Gala") | Event trademarks are actively enforced | Coachella sued Urban Outfitters over "Coachella" listings (settled 2017) and won a permanent injunction against "Filmchella" ([WIPR](https://www.worldipreview.com/news/coachella-settles-trademark-clash-with-urban-outfitters-14686); [Fox Rothschild](https://advertisinglaw.foxrothschild.com/2018/10/coachella-filmchella-dispute-ends/)); the Met filed a MET GALA application in 2020, status unverified ([Justia](https://trademarks.justia.com/885/96/met-88596707.html)) | "Desert festival weekend," "Gala night," "Red carpet season" |
| "Dupe" of a named brand | False advertising and affiliation claims are rising | Lululemon v. Costco (settled 2026, no merits ruling); Lululemon registered LULULEMON DUPE; Williams-Sonoma v. Quince targeted comparison charts ([Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/october/lululemon-dupe); [TFL](https://www.thefashionlaw.com/false-advertising-comes-under-fire-in-new-dupe-lawsuits/)) | "Get the look for less," "Recreate the trend"; no designer names, logos or brand-versus-brand quality claims |
| Recreate a celebrity's look | Right of publicity and false endorsement | In the Fortnite emote suits most claims failed, but a false endorsement claim survived in *Pellegrino* ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/fortnite-sparks-a-battle-royale-over-the-right-to-use-dance-moves.html)); UK passing off in *Fenty v Arcadia* ([National Archives](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2015/3)) | Describe the trend, not the person; no celebrity photos |
| Real brand names on items | Low with consent | Nominative, truthful references are generally allowed if they don't imply sponsorship ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/06/imitation-game-legal-considerations-with-dupes-based-business-models)) | Use only partner or feed-licensed brands; render logos only with consent |

Dupe framing also has a commercial risk. A mid-market partner may not want its item labeled as a cheaper copy of a designer piece. Avoid fast-fashion marketplace partners with heavy IP litigation, such as Shein, which faces designer copying suits and fought Temu over about 2,300 product images ([TIME](https://time.com/6295035/shein-lawsuit-copyright-infringement); [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/shein-temu-london-copyright-trial)). Require an IP indemnity in any brand or feed contract [lawyer].

## 18+ is cheapest to run, and 16+ buys less relief than it looks

The 2025–26 wave of law moved the compliance burden from under-13s to all minors. The amended COPPA rule has applied in full since **22 Apr 2026** ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)). Texas's app-store age law is in effect after the Fifth Circuit stayed the injunction, and Utah, Louisiana, Alabama and California versions arrive by 2027 ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251111-texas-targets-app-stores-with-new-accountability-law); [Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/05/update-on-utah-app-store-law--another-waiting-game)). These laws send apps an age signal from the store. Once you receive a signal that a user is 13–15 or 16–17, you have actual knowledge and must act on it. California's Age-Appropriate Design Code, which covers under-18s, is partly enforceable since March 2026 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/ninth-circuit-issues-mixed-ruling-on-california-age-appropriate-design-code-act)). Florida's under-16 social-media law is back in effect, though whether a styling game counts as "social media" is unclear [lawyer] ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/10/federal-appeals-court-finds-florida-social-media-ban-for-minors-likely-constitutional/)). The House-passed federal KIDS Act is **not law** as of Nov 2026 ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/29/kids-bill-faces-uncertainty-after-house-passage/)). In the UK and EU, regulators now say self-declared ages are not enough: Ofcom expects "highly effective" age checks, and the EU's DSA guidelines call self-declaration inappropriate ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/keep-underage-children-off-your-platforms-ofcom-tells-tech-firms); [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/digital-services-act/european-commission-makes-new-announcements-on-the-protection-of-minors-under-the-digital-services-act/)).

| Area | 16+ launch | 18+ launch |
|---|---|---|
| COPPA (under-13) | Out of scope unless you learn of under-13 users | Same |
| California design code (under-18) | In scope | Largely out if age is credibly enforced |
| App-store age-signal laws | Must block 13–15 and apply teen defaults for 16–17 | Block all minor signals |
| FTC teen purchase expectations (Genshin order: parental consent under 16) | Lower risk | Lowest |
| CCPA opt-in to sell data of 13–15s | Not needed | Not needed |
| UK Online Safety Act / ICO Children's Code (under-18) | **In scope** | Out only with highly effective age checks |
| EU DSA Art. 28 and proposed Digital Fairness Act minors rules | **In scope** | Out only with effective age verification |
| Voting and profile design | Teen-safe defaults for 16–17 | Adult standards |

Monetization rules are tightening around the concept's exact features. The FTC took **$245M in refunds from Epic** over dark-pattern purchases ([Kelley Drye](https://kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/podcasts/ad-law-access/two-epic-cases-from-the-ftc)). It fined HoYoverse **$20M** over confusing multi-currency exchange rates, requiring parental consent for under-16 purchases ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/rpg/ftc-says-genshin-impact-deceived-children-and-orders-its-publisher-to-pay-a-usd20-million-fine-and-stop-selling-loot-boxes-to-kids)). EU consumer authorities expect virtual-currency prices to be shown in real money and coin packs sized so players aren't forced to overbuy ([Linklaters](https://techinsights.linklaters.com/post/102k6t4/game-changer-eu-introduces-consumer-protection-guidance-for-in-game-virtual-curr)). The EU's **Digital Fairness Act, proposed 11 Nov 2026 and still pending**, would ban paid loot boxes and pay-to-win or pay-to-progress for minors. It would also switch paid currencies off by default where a provider is reasonably certain minors play, and let players disable streak penalties ([Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/28479/european-commission-proposes-ban-on-paid-loot-boxes-and-pay-to-win-for-minors)).

Rewarded sharing runs into hard platform rules. Meta's developer policy bars incentivizing people to post to Facebook, but allows rewards for friends who install through a referral ([Meta](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/apps/policies/policies-deep-dive/)). Apple bars making users rate, review or do other tasks to unlock features ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google treats incentivized installs and reviews as manipulation ([Android Developers Blog](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2017/06/google-plays-policy-on-incentivized.html)). Under FTC guidance, a rewarded post is an endorsement that needs a disclosure, and affiliate links need clear "we earn a commission" labels ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-combat-deceptive-reviews-endorsements)).

## What this means for the concept

### Design choices: what the evidence supports, challenges or needs changing

| Design choice | Verdict | Why | Suggested adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real mid-market items at real prices | **Supported** | Open lane; Gen Z trading down; no 1:1 price game found | Show "price at last check"; keep sold-out items wearable |
| Body type set by the client brief | **Supported, costly** | Clearest differentiator; competitors' size inclusion is late and thin | Start with 4–5 bodies and a small catalog; budget rendering per item × body |
| Player voting as the core judge | **Supported** | AI-trust gap; voting is the category's hook | Make vote fairness the product: see next row |
| Stranger voting vs friend voting | **Stranger at launch, friends in V2** | Gen Z trusts friends most, but friend voting doesn't scale at launch and produces cliques; anonymous strangers produced spite-voting in Dress to Impress | Anonymous head-to-head pairs, voter-reputation weighting, no downvotes or public low scores, avatar-only looks; add "ask my friends" polls later |
| Coins at 1:1 with item prices | **Supported, with a labeling risk** | Teaches real budgeting | A "45-coin" item that costs $45 at retail, while 45 bought coins might cost $0.45, invites confusion with regulators' real-money display rules. Label retail price and coin cost separately, and keep one paid currency |
| Coin packs sold while coins are "tight" | **Challenged** | Bought coins buy more closet, and Covet players already see closet size as pay-to-win; the proposed EU law targets pay-to-progress for minors | Make budget-capped challenges the main ranked mode, so closet size can't decide outcomes; sell cosmetics and convenience over power |
| Limited wears plus paid wear refills | **Challenged** | Teaches cost-per-wear, but works like an energy system (the model behind Kim Kardashian: Hollywood) and could read as pay-to-progress | Let wears regenerate free over time; sell refills only as convenience; no punishing streak loss |
| Season pass, cosmetics, no pay-to-win votes | **Supported** | Matches what players accept; avoids the Nikki backlash | Clear auto-renew terms, easy refunds; no randomized paid items ever |
| Affiliate links as secondary revenue | **Supported** | Cents per user vs dollars from IAP; French Connection's 110K clicks yielded $12K in sales | Treat clicks as ROI proof for brand deals; label every shoppable card; never award coins for clicks or purchases |
| Brand-sponsored challenges | **Supported, later** | Drest's main revenue; brands want measurable Gen Z reach | Start with free exposure swaps (Covet model) to build the catalog; charge once you can show engagement and clicks |
| Aggregated trend data for brands | **Defer** | No precedent; minors' data rules; tiny sample early | Bundle insight with sponsored challenges; exclude anyone under 18 or flagged by store signals; aggregate only |
| Event and dupe challenges | **Supported with safe naming** | Event marks and dupe claims are actively litigated | "Desert festival," "Gala night," "Get the look for less"; no celebrity names, photos or designer logos |
| Free wears for linking socials or sharing | **Change** | Rewarding posts breaks Meta policy and needs FTC disclosure | Sharing free and unrewarded, with an automatic app watermark; reward the referrer when a friend installs and finishes onboarding; never reward reviews [lawyer] |
| Influencer-first growth | **Supported** | Dress to Impress grew through TikTok and streamers; 59% of game IAP comes from paid installs, so organic reach matters | Influencer content needs #ad disclosure; plan a paid-acquisition budget anyway |
| 16+ vs 18+ | **18+ (or a 17+ store rating) first, especially in UK/EU** | 16+ removes US under-16 burdens but keeps every under-18 regime, and the proposed EU law would switch off paid coins for minors | 18+ with store age signals in UK/EU; if 16+ in the US, apply teen-safe defaults to anyone flagged 16–17; build teen mode as a separate configuration |
| V2 real closet, friends, borrowing | **Supported direction, crowded** | Whering (10M users) and Alta own the closet space | Differentiate on judging and friend challenges, not AI closet features |

### Opportunities and white space

The clearest white space is **fair, structured human judging of looks built for real bodies and real budgets**. AI companies generate outfits, games offer fantasy items or luxury, and closet apps don't judge. Within that, three specific openings stand out. The first is the client-brief format ("petite, job interview, $150"), which nobody runs. The second is dupe and trend-recreation challenges at real prices, which fit Gen Z's trading-down mood. The third is a brand-facing measurement story: real items, real prices and click data give mid-market brands a cleaner ROI proof than a Roblox world can. A stranded audience also exists. Polyvore's former users and Shuffles' collage makers show demand for styling as creation, and Covet's aging base is underserved.

### Key risks

| Risk | Severity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Retention | High | The median game keeps under 1% of players at day 30; the plan needs top-10% retention (about 4%) |
| Asset production cost | High | Items × body types × refresh cadence; brand opt-in needed to modify images |
| Small niche revenue ceiling | High | Covet-scale US revenue is about $8–12M a year (estimate) and shrinking |
| Voting abuse | Medium-high | Spite, cliques and bots break trust; this is the top complaint in the category |
| Paid user acquisition costs | Medium-high | Most game revenue depends on paid installs, which favors studios with capital |
| Regulatory drift toward 18 | Medium | Age signals, UK/EU age checks, proposed EU minors' bans |
| IP and naming | Medium | Event marks, dupe claims, celebrity likeness, unlicensed image modification |
| Fast followers | Medium | EA could add body briefs to Covet; Pinterest or Alta could add voting |

### Defensibility

The mechanics are easy to copy, so the moat has to be built from things that compound. The first is the **community and its judging reputation**: a voter-reputation score, stylist ranks and a known fair system take time to build and are what players lose if they leave. The second is **brand catalog rights**: written permissions to render items, which a copycat has to negotiate one brand at a time. The third is a **taste dataset**, showing which items and combinations win for which bodies and budgets. That data has value for brands once there is scale. Polyvore's collapse adds one more lesson: player trust is an asset, so let players export their looks, and don't sell out the community for shopping traffic.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes the concept's center of gravity. The real product is not "Covet with cheaper clothes." It is a **trust machine for taste**, and its hardest engineering problem is fair voting, not rendering outfits. Budget-capped, body-specific briefs judged by reputation-weighted anonymous voters solve two problems at once: they fix the category's top complaint, and they defuse the pay-to-win concern that regulators are now writing into law. That suggests a sharper sequencing. Prove first that strangers' votes feel meaningful, on a small catalog with a few bodies and brands that opted in. Then add friends, brand sponsorships and the real closet.

The business case is viable but modest unless the game breaks out of the niche. The realistic path to a "real business" is IAP plus brand partnerships, with affiliate links and trend data mainly serving as proof to brands. Launching 18+ (or 17+) keeps the compliance load manageable for a non-engineer founder, though it means giving up part of the younger Gen Z audience at first. The strongest long-term asset is the dataset of which looks win for which bodies and budgets. AI companies need that human judgment and currently can't get it, so it could position the game as the validation layer the AI shopping wave lacks.
